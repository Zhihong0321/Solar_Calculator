⚡ How Battery Savings Really Work

Why changing battery efficiency barely moves your final bill — explained from first principles.

1 Where the battery enters the calculation

The solar calculator first figures out how much solar you generate and how much you use in the morning (direct self-consumption). The battery only touches what's left over.

Key point: The battery never creates energy. It only redirects leftover solar that would otherwise be exported to the grid at a low rate (~RM 0.27/kWh).

2 The three limits on battery charging

Every day, the battery can only charge up to the smallest of three limits:

CAP 1
Available excess solar
How much leftover solar you actually have per day
CAP 2
Battery physical size
e.g. 16 kWh battery with 5% DoD = 15.2 kWh usable
CAP 3
Night usage requirement
How much energy you actually need at night ÷ efficiency
daily charge = min( CAP1, CAP2, CAP3 )
where CAP3 = night usage ÷ round-trip efficiency
This is the key: CAP3 grows when efficiency drops. Lower efficiency → system tries to pull MORE solar to compensate.

4 Why the final savings barely change

Total monthly savings has three parts:

Total Savings = Bill Reduction + EEI Saving + Export Credit

When you lower battery efficiency but there's enough excess solar:

Component0% Loss20% LossChange
Energy delivered to home 456 kWh 456 kWh Same ✓
Bill Reduction RM 228 RM 228 Same ✓
Solar consumed by battery 456 kWh 570 kWh +114 kWh
Export Credit RM 28 RM 0 −RM 28
Total Savings RM 256 RM 228 −RM 28 (11%)
The only thing that drops is export credit — the cheapest component. The expensive grid power you avoid buying stays exactly the same. That's why the total barely moves.

5 When efficiency actually matters

Efficiency only hurts you when excess solar runs out before the battery can compensate:

HURTS Small system + big battery + high night usage
HURTS Very high morning usage (little leftover solar)
NO EFFECT Well-sized system with plenty of excess solar
NO EFFECT Battery already covers all night usage

6 The mathematical proof

Here's exactly what happens step by step:

Given: excessSolar = 18.7 kWh/day, nightUse = 25.3 kWh/day, battery = 15.2 kWh

At 0% loss (efficiency = 1.00):
cap3 = 25.3 ÷ 1.00 = 25.3
input = min(18.7, 15.2, 25.3) = 15.2 (battery-limited)
delivered = 15.2 × 1.00 = 15.2 kWh

At 20% loss (efficiency = 0.80):
cap3 = 25.3 ÷ 0.80 = 31.7 ← grew!
input = min(18.7, 17.0, 31.7) = 17.0 (still battery-limited)
delivered = 17.0 × 0.80 = 13.6 kWh ← drops 10%

At 0% loss with MORE solar (40 kWh/day excess):
cap3 = 25.3 ÷ 1.00 = 25.3
input = min(40, 15.2, 25.3) = 15.2
delivered = 15.2 kWh

At 20% loss with MORE solar (40 kWh/day excess):
cap3 = 25.3 ÷ 0.80 = 31.7
input = min(40, 17.0, 31.7) = 17.0
delivered = 17.0 × 0.80 = 13.6 kWh

At 20% loss with ABUNDANT solar (50+ kWh/day):
cap3 = 31.7
input = min(50, 17.0, 31.7) = 17.0
delivered = 17.0 × 0.80 = 13.6 kWh
← Battery size is now the hard ceiling. Efficiency loss is real.
Bottom line: The system is mathematically sound. The "compensation" effect is not a bug — it's the correct behavior of a system where the battery is sized smaller than the available excess solar. When excess solar is abundant, efficiency losses are absorbed by drawing more from the solar pool.

❓ Common Questions

"Is the calculator cheating by ignoring losses?" +
No. Losses are fully accounted for. At 20% loss, the system draws 17.0 kWh from solar to deliver 13.6 kWh to the home — the 3.4 kWh difference is the loss. But because there's enough excess solar, the delivered amount to the home only drops slightly, and the bill reduction stays nearly the same.
"Why doesn't 20% loss mean 20% less savings?" +
Because 20% loss means you need 25% more solar input (1 ÷ 0.80 = 1.25) to get the same output. If that extra solar is available, the output stays the same. Only when solar runs out does the loss translate 1:1 into reduced savings. Think of it like a water tank: a leaky pipe needs more water in, but if your well has plenty, the bucket at the end still gets filled.
"So battery efficiency doesn't matter at all?" +
It matters, but in a specific way. Lower efficiency means more solar is consumed (less exported), so your export credit drops. It also means you hit the solar-availability ceiling sooner. For a well-sized system with good sun, the practical impact on your wallet is small — typically RM 20–50/month difference between best and worst efficiency.
"What should I tell customers who doubt this?" +
Show them this page. The math is transparent: min(excess solar, battery size, night usage ÷ efficiency). The efficiency term only bites when it pushes the night-usage cap above the available solar. In most well-designed systems, there's enough headroom that efficiency variations are absorbed. This is standard engineering — not a trick.
